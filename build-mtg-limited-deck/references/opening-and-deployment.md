# Opening hands and deployment plans

## Mulligan profile

Test representative hands against the proposed list: a functional curve, two lands with a missing color, a land-heavy hand, and a hand containing the expensive payoff or synergy package being evaluated. Use actual pool cards and quantities. Label chosen examples as illustrative; estimate their frequency only with an explicit sampling model.

For each relevant hand, specify play/draw, hand size, the applicable mulligan rule, usable colors, the first meaningful board or interaction play, and the draws required to continue. Compare keeping with the card cost and likely functionality of a smaller hand. For a London mulligan, choose the bottom card from the seven actually considered and re-evaluate the resulting hand. Keep unseen redraws outside the comparison.

Separate a two-land hand with castable selection or landcycling from one whose early spells all need the missing color. Price the fixing spell's mana and lost development turn. Extra draw steps on the draw help access but come with the opponent's initiative. Count castable early answers and bodies rather than applying a universal land-count rule. Use matchup knowledge only when available before the decision.

Identify hands this deck can afford to keep because of cheap plays, redundant sources, or recovery tools, and hands its expensive cards cannot rescue in time. If many plausible hands depend on finding one color or a particular early draw, revisit sources, tapped lands, curve, or package size. A single awkward sample establishes a failure mode, not its frequency.

For numerical estimates, state sources, total lands, cards seen, play/draw, and mulligan/hand-generation assumptions. Treat Arena hand smoothing as a modeling uncertainty unless its relevant behavior is verified; distinguish seeing a source from casting the needed sequence.

## Spending mana across the game

Build feasible sequences from representative hands through turns two to five and the first major payoff. Include a missed land drop or missing-color branch when it affects the candidate ranking. Use the resource-dependency and mana sections of the decision framework for costs and conditional sources.

Compare what the mana produces: attackers, blockers, removed obstacles, cards that can soon be cast, or progress toward an engine win. Evaluate both unused mana and mana fully spent on low-impact actions while stronger plays remain in hand. A cheap cantrip or repeatable activation fills the curve only when it advances the plan without displacing needed development.

Test these competing sequences where the cards support them:

- Deploy a threat versus draw or activate an engine; name the blocker, attack, or activation each line gains by the next turn.
- Cast one expensive threat versus two cheaper spells; retain the appropriate colored mana for interaction or protection when that improves the position.
- Cast a setup permanent before the spell it discounts or rewards; count tokens, haste, prowess, and loyalty-generated mana at their actual timing.
- Use a mana sink at the last useful window while preserving a credible response; include the mana source tapped to activate a utility land in its opportunity cost.

Count each source and each once-per-turn activation once. Reserve interaction mana only after paying setup and development costs, including required colors. Distinguish repeatable sinks, conditional sinks, and card flow that itself needs more mana to convert into a board.

Prefer the package that produces useful turns across plausible draws. Support claims about a mana-intensive format with current evidence; keep the objective winning the game rather than maximizing mana spent. Recommend extra cheap cards, lands, or sinks only when they address a demonstrated role or consistency problem.

## Deploying threats through interaction

For each important threat or engine, describe its return if it resolves, is countered, is removed immediately, or loses its token to bounce. Distinguish cast triggers from entry triggers and persistent value from value that needs an untap. Check whether recursion actually recovers that card from the relevant zone.

Give a practical deployment plan: exploit an opponent's unavailable answer mana, lead with an independently useful threat, deploy while retaining protection, or wait to double-spell. Verify which removal or counterspell classes can answer each threat. Protection against a spell targeting a permanent usually does not protect the creature spell on the stack.

Price waiting in lost attacks, blockers, engine activations, and the opponent's additional draws or mana. Explain what concrete improvement waiting buys. Treat bait as a branch: the opponent can save an answer for the better threat. Prefer threat redundancy and useful outcomes when the bait is ignored. Avoid claiming safety from untapped-land counts alone; include nonland and alternative payment resources.

Use these comparisons to assess the pool's threat mix, recovery, and protection slots. Reserve opponent-specific hand inference for replay evidence. A powerful card being answered is not by itself a construction failure; ask what the deck can deploy next and whether the exchange advances its winning plan.
