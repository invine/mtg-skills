## Risk when ahead or behind

Judge advantage by the projected winning route across board, hand, life, mana, engines, and remaining library. Establish whose position improves with time. A life or material lead can coexist with a losing race or an opposing engine that will take over. Reassess the role after a meaningful exchange or new information.

- **Ahead with a durable winning route — ask how we lose.** Identify the opponent's credible comeback routes and what resources prevent them. Prefer converting the existing advantage when extra damage or material adds little to winning but exposes a key blocker, threat, or protection spell. Consider preserving redundancy or accepting a modest material loss to close a dangerous route. Trade or simplify only if the resulting position still supports our win.
- **Behind on the current trajectory — ask how we win.** Work backward from reachable outs, attack windows, and sequences that can reverse the game. Accept a narrower success branch or vulnerability to an answer when the safer-looking line mostly postpones defeat. Preserve the mana, life, and bodies needed to exploit the out if it arrives. Compare multiple routes before committing to one; require a concrete improvement in winning chances to justify added risk.
- **Temporary advantage or unclear role — compare both clocks.** Charge caution for the extra draws, blockers, or engine activations it grants. An attack can reduce overall risk by ending the game sooner; a low life total can still permit patient defense when our late game is stronger. Recompute the full position instead of equating ahead with passive or behind with reckless.

Use the inferred hand range to price credible dangers. Establish what playing around each answer costs and whether it actually improves our outcome against that answer. Preserve a verified win; spend resources on insurance when it protects meaningful winning chances, rather than on accumulating an unnecessary margin or fearing every legal card.

**Complete when:** the comparison names our winning route, the opponent's relevant out, and how the chosen risk improves the outcome through the next meaningful conversion of resources.

Strategy references: Reid Duke, [Playing From Ahead, Playing From Behind](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/playing-ahead-playing-behind-2015-03-30) and [Playing Safe and Playing Scared](https://magic.wizards.com/en/news/feature/playing-safe-and-playing-scared-2015-08-24).

## Interaction resolution

For a verdict that depends on a death, trigger, threshold, or loyalty change, read the full text of the relevant permanents, including static abilities on other objects. Pay costs and order cast/target triggers correctly. After each resolution, recompute stats and resources, apply state-based actions, and stack newly triggered abilities before the next priority window. Check whether a continuous effect changes the usual result, such as keeping a planeswalker on the battlefield at zero loyalty; remove that exception when its source leaves.

For a returning permanent, establish its new-object status, tapped state, counters, preparation, and activation eligibility. Distinguish casting from entering: a cast-dependent entry trigger may not recur, and a previous exile link may no longer provide access to the stolen card. Identify which value survives before recommending a trade or recursion loop.

## Alternative sequences

Start both lines from the same verified state. Write the actions in order, paying exact colored costs and updating the battlefield after each resolution. Reserve mana for a response only if it remains available after development and selection costs. Check land drops, once-per-turn loyalty or other activation limits, summoning sickness, and trigger timing.

Carry both lines through a sensible opposing attack or answer. Assign actual blockers to attackers and attack targets; account separately for player life and planeswalker survival. A spare creature can buy a turn without protecting every permanent. For a recurring token line, track the loyalty or other finite resource across the turns claimed.

Branch at new information. Moving a draw past a mill, shuffle, search, or selection effect can change the card drawn; use plausible draws from that point rather than importing the observed later card. Distinguish a line available before the draw from a response chosen after seeing it. Keep the comparison useful by explaining what each branch preserves and what it gives up.

Practice cue: **After the entire sequence, what mana, blockers, and activations are actually left?**

## Combat audit

For each relevant combat, including a decision to pass, establish legal attackers and blockers from actual abilities, temporary restrictions, summoning sickness, and tapped state. Compare the played line with the strongest useful attack subset, attack targets, or block assignment. Check missed profitable attacks, extra attackers that expose an engine, and trades that spend a future winning threat merely to save expendable life.

For each important candidate and credible response, assign legal blocks and resolve relevant tricks or activations. Calculate damage that reaches each attacked player or planeswalker, resulting life or loyalty, creatures lost or surviving, and interaction spent. Account for evasion, vigilance, trample, first/double strike, prevention, and damage assignment where relevant. Treat total attacking power as an input; establish damage dealt after blocks. Use the opponent's strategic incentives to rank responses: preserving an engine can justify taking more damage now.

Evaluate counter placement and precombat development separately, then combine them with the attack into a legal sequence. Check whether another counter changes a kill or survival threshold, the likely block, or damage getting through. If either size still trades under the opponent's credible response, compare retaining the counter on another body with the extra damage if unblocked. Distinguish surviving a single block from surviving a double block.

Give a positional reason to trade or preserve each contested body: a scarce evasive clock, a pump recipient, a protector of repeat activations, an expended value creature, or replaceable material. Judge trades from both players' resulting plans, using the opponent's inferred range and incentives. A token is not free simply because it did not originate as a card in hand. Equal card counts need not mean equal winning chances.

State which attack or block is preferred, why the opponent's credible response leaves that line better, and the risk that remains. When extra damage leaves lethal on the same turn, compare it with the protection surrendered rather than treating damage as an automatic improvement.

## Removal versus trading: identify the scarce resource

Compare the resulting board and next attack, including graveyard value and retained resources. Count useful threats separately from cards in hand. A nominal two-for-one can reduce winning chances if it leaves the player with answers but no clock.

Worked pattern from the discussion: the player has a 3/2 flyer and a 3/4 ground threat, two removal spells, a flashback pump, and no fresh threat. The opponent has a 4/1 and a 1/1. Exiling the 4/1 lets the ground attacker kill the blocking 1/1 and survive, keeping both attackers. Pumping both attackers by +1/+1 instead can let a double block trade both defenders for the ground threat, leaving only the flyer. Compare the one extra point of flying damage and saved removal against losing threat redundancy, spending flashback, and any value from putting the 4/1 in the graveyard. If the flyer is answered, two removal spells cannot maintain pressure by themselves.

The transferable question is: **What useful board do I retain if their next card answers my best threat?** Favor the line supported by the actual answer, not an automatic rule to spend or save removal.

## Development versus incremental value: compare clocks

Identify which opposing permanent actually obstructs the winning route. Removing a ground 1/4 and a potential token may be less valuable than freeing a flyer from an opposing exile enchantment when the player has safe life and an aerial clock. Check whether sequencing entry triggers and counters creates a larger flyer before declaring the alternatives equivalent.

Trace the next two turns under plausible opposing development. State that a faster clock pressures an engine or expensive threat; claim it prevents that threat only if timing and known information establish this. Keep different games and their threats separate when recalling examples.

Compare attacking now and waiting over the same relevant turns. If they take damage now, retain the surviving attacker for the next attack, while pricing its temporary absence as a blocker. If they trade, remove both exchanged bodies before evaluating the next combat. Count damage getting through the remaining blockers using the combat procedure above, including newly generated tokens, plausible development, available pump mana, and which creatures will be ready. Charge waiting for the opposing engine's extra resources and blockers; credit it for our improved stats and interaction. Establish any predicted trade from those future stats rather than assuming today's exchange remains available.

For a close comparison, show a compact branch table: **response and likelihood → damage now → damage on the next relevant attack → surviving threats/blockers → resources spent**. Keep player damage and planeswalker damage separate. Compare cumulative damage over the shared horizon and the board left afterward; fewer attackers can deal more damage through fewer blockers. A zero-damage trade can still improve the winning route by removing an engine or opening later attacks. Use conditional totals or ranges when responses differ; calculate probability-weighted damage only with defensible branch probabilities.

Test whether extra damage advances lethal or forces a meaningful concession. If it leaves the clock unchanged, price the lost blocker and planeswalker exposure explicitly. Count the full value of protected loyalty: a token, selection, and another trigger or counter can depend on the same activation. Deduct mana spent re-empowering it from the next development or combat plan.

For a fragile engine, write the earliest payoff turn at the current mana. Compare its interrupted floor and setup exposure with the useful body or defense given up now. A two-turn ceiling is not an immediate return, especially when a needed blocker must be developed first.

## Baiting interaction and concentrated threats

Evaluate removal bait against the option of preserving the offered creatures. Count the attack power, evasion, and future pump recipients being risked; explain which later threat benefits if the answer is spent. Check whether the opponent can decline the exchange, use a cheaper public activation, or retain another relevant answer. Forcing a profitable use of removal does not itself establish that the bait improves our position.

Include removal already in our hand when assessing enemy growth. Sacrificing several bodies into a single threat can improve the value of a disabling answer. Price the answer's mana and timing alongside the creatures lost in combat and the delay to our next threat. Keep that attractive branch proportional to how likely the opponent is to choose it; a smaller investment may already accomplish their goal.

## Exposed tokens versus durable development

Price publicly available interaction into the line. If a six-mana play produces a small body and a large token, a visible cheap bounce spell may erase most of its immediate value. Two smaller real creatures can retain more material through the same bounce and leave the expensive play available later.

Count attackers and blockers after the response. A better material outcome can still fail to save a planeswalker. Explain exactly what the alternative preserves rather than upgrading “better” into “saves everything.”

## Attack windows and hidden interaction

First enumerate mana sources and their colors, then the legal responses in the format. One untapped green-blue land represents green tricks as well as blue bounce. Account for relevant prowess triggers, including multiple instances, when calculating survival. Distinguish tapping lands from exhausting all usable resources.

Compare the risk of acting now with the risk of letting the opponent untap and develop. A favorable attack window may justify exposure to a narrow trick when waiting worsens the position. List materially plausible punishments and their effects; require a defensible prior and model before attaching percentages to an unknown hand.

Practice cue: **What changes if they get one more untap?**

## Removal timing and activated protection

Identify when the answer stops working: permanent growth, protection becoming available, or a target leaving range. Resolve intervening triggers before calculating removal. Casting a cheap spell can trigger opposing lifegain and then a counter before a toughness-reduction spell resolves; include ward or other payment costs in the complete sequence.

Compare removal plus affordable development now with the strongest development line and removal later. If several permanents form a growth engine, compare removing the payoff with removing the enabler. Price the lost development and the possibility that waiting makes either target unanswerable. Use known growth and public abilities to establish urgency; later-drawn equipment or tricks only illustrate the risk.

Read the full activation cost, including resources outside lands. An untapped mana artifact can supply the mana for a creature's protection ability and then be sacrificed to pay the same ability's artifact-sacrifice cost, if the rules and text permit it. “All lands tapped” therefore does not establish that removal is safe.

For a Puppet Beast-like position, verify whether a remaining artifact enables a counter and hexproof before proposing earlier removal. Compare that state with the later turn where the artifact or mana is actually unavailable. A tapped creature may still activate abilities whose costs do not require tapping it.

Practice cue: **Can every part of their activation cost be paid from the whole battlefield?**

## Concessions and low-equity games

Evaluate a credible multi-turn winning route through the opponent's board, hand size, engines, recursion, and plausible future draws. Distinguish drawing an answer from stabilizing, and stabilizing from eventually winning. A comfortable life total or remaining bomb alone establishes neither.

If winning requires several particular draws while an opponent with a full hand finds no useful spell for many turns, classify the position as very low equity rather than treating every live draw as a realistic out. State residual possibilities without inventing a zero-percent or precise win rate. Account for match stakes and the user's willingness to spend time pursuing remote chances when evaluating the concession.

## Reopening a disputed finding

Locate the exact game and before-action state. Re-read the relevant card text, rebuild the proposed line, and test the user's line against the same responses. State the factual correction and how it changes the strategic conclusion. Preserve the distinction between an accepted sequencing error and an unresolved strategic disagreement.

Rebuild one comparison incorporating all accumulated corrections before issuing a new preference. Challenge the proposed replacement as carefully as the played line, including its opportunity cost and the opponent's best responses. An acceptable outcome is not proof that a line beats the strongest alternative. When the preference changes, identify the factual correction, omitted cost, or revised weighting; when it remains close, retain that uncertainty.

Maintain a short working ledger of the current verdict, supporting evidence, confidence, unresolved branches, and withdrawn claims during a long review. Carry accepted corrections forward without treating earlier conclusions as new evidence.
